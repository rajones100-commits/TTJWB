Drop-off rates in youth participation in sport

Exploring when participation changes, the barriers behind it and what could help more young people remain involved.

01 / Know the terms

What counts as a drop-off?

A young person can leave a club yet remain active in other ways. These terms help us ask the right question.

Participation

Taking part

Doing sport or physical activity at a particular time. Surveys may measure club sport, informal sport or all physical activity differently.

Drop-off

Taking part less often

A reduction in how frequently someone participates. Comparing different age groups can suggest a pattern, but cannot show that the same people reduced their activity.

Dropout

Stopping an activity

Leaving a specific sport, club or programme. This does not automatically mean someone has stopped all sport or exercise.

Reading the numbers correctly

Lower participation among older pupils is evidence of an age-related pattern. Measuring how many individuals actually stop requires following the same people over time or asking about their past and current participation.

03 / The barriers

Why might participation change?

Research points to several overlapping reasons. They affect different young people in different ways.

01 / ACCESS

Cost and transport

Fees, equipment and travel can make opportunities harder to reach. Gwent peer research also emphasised safe, local venues. StreetGames · Gwent research

02 / TIME

Other demands

Study, work, other interests and the commitment required by formal sessions can compete with sport. StreetGames · Gwent research

03 / EXPERIENCE

Confidence and belonging

Confidence, body image and feeling comfortable in a session matter, particularly in research about teenage girls. Friends can make trying an activity easier. Women in Sport · Youth Sport Trust · Gwent research

04 / CHOICE

One format does not suit everyone

Some young people value competition; others want informal play, different sports or more freedom to choose. Gwent research

04 / Why it matters

What could be lost?

Sport can provide movement, friendship, enjoyment and a sense of achievement. Leaving a club does not necessarily mean losing all of those benefits; the question is whether a young person finds another way to be active.

Physical health

Less opportunity to move

If overall activity falls, it may be harder to reach the UK Chief Medical Officers’ guidance for ages 5–18: an average of at least 60 minutes of moderate to vigorous activity a day across the week. Read guidance ↗

Wellbeing

Fewer positive experiences

Sport and activity can support wellbeing and confidence. The effect of leaving will depend on the person and what activities replace it. Read guidance ↗

Connection

Less time with a sporting community

Young people in Gwent described friendships and belonging as valuable parts of sport. Leaving a team can mean losing that particular social space. Read research ↗

05 / Responses

What could help young people stay?

These are practical options drawn from the barriers and preferences in the research. They are ideas to test locally, not proven fixes for every young person.

Option 1

Make it easier to get there

Use accessible local venues and consider the combined cost of sessions, kit and travel.

Option 2

Offer more than one way to play

Keep competitive pathways while adding informal, social or flexible sessions.

Option 3

Ask young people first

Find out which times, activities and session styles they would actually choose; involve them in shaping the offer.

Question to investigate: “If you played sport at 14–15, do you still take part now? If your participation changed, what made the biggest difference?” Ask separately about club sport, informal sport and other physical activity.
06 / What is being done

UK and Welsh action

Sport Wales

Uses the School Sport Survey to understand young people’s experiences and help organisations plan opportunities. Its strategy calls for flexible opportunities through life transitions. Evidence ↗ · Strategy ↗

StreetGames in Wales

Has worked with young peer researchers in Gwent and on the Us Girls Wales project to listen to young people and shape local activities around their preferences. Gwent ↗ · Us Girls Wales ↗

Women in Sport and Youth Sport Trust

Publish research on girls’ experiences, confidence and barriers, giving schools and providers evidence to consider when designing sessions. Women in Sport ↗ · Youth Sport Trust ↗

Resource 1 · Sport Wales

School Sport Survey 2022

This source has good currency because it was published in 2022 and covers the 2021/22 academic year. It is highly relevant because it examines participation among pupils in Wales, including Years 10 and 11, although it does not follow individuals to establish exactly how many stopped. Its authority is strong because Sport Wales is the national organisation responsible for developing sport in Wales. Its accuracy is supported by a large national survey and clear definitions, but the results are self-reported. Its purpose is to inform schools and sporting organisations and improve opportunities, rather than sell a product, although Sport Wales has an interest in encouraging participation.

How it helps: It shows how participation changes across school years and provides a Welsh starting point for investigating what happens after Year 11. View source details ↓

Resource 2 · Women in Sport

Reframing Sport for Teenage Girls

This source has reasonable currency because it was published in 2022 using research conducted in July 2021. It is extremely relevant because it covers ages 11–18 and gives separate findings for 17–18 year olds, although its main focus is girls. Its authority is strong because Women in Sport specialises in girls’ participation and the survey was conducted by professional researchers at Savanta. Its accuracy is supported by a nationally representative survey of 2,291 girls and 2,024 boys, although a term such as “sporty” is based on personal identity rather than an objective participation measure. Its purpose is to raise awareness and encourage improved provision. This campaigning aim may shape the presentation, but the claims are supported by survey evidence.

How it helps: It allows comparisons between girls and boys and supports questions about compulsory PE, confidence, body image and available activities. View source details ↓

Resource 3 · StreetGames

1,000 Young Voices

This source has good currency because the survey and follow-up discussions took place in 2023. It is relevant because it investigates barriers to sport and includes 16–19 year olds, although the headline statistics cover the wider 11–24 range and lower-income households. Its authority comes from StreetGames’ experience in underserved communities and its use of Platypus Research. Its accuracy is strengthened by a survey of 1,000 young people and a qualitative forum, but its sample should not be treated as representative of every UK teenager. Its purpose is to amplify lower-income young people’s voices and help providers improve opportunities. StreetGames has an interest in highlighting barriers, but this is made clear.

How it helps: Its findings can shape questionnaire choices about cost, time, confidence, energy, anxiety and having someone to attend with. View source details ↓

Resource 4 · Youth Sport Trust

Girls Active National Reports 2025

This source has excellent currency because responses were collected in autumn 2024 and summer 2025. It is relevant because it examines participation, confidence and experiences of physical activity, but does not calculate a 16–18 dropout rate. Its authority is strong because the Youth Sport Trust is an established charity specialising in PE and school sport. Its accuracy benefits from 17,971 responses across 137 English schools, although the data was unweighted and relates to England rather than Wales. Its purpose is to identify inequalities, improve girls’ experiences and promote Girls Active, making it both informative and campaigning.

How it helps: It supports investigation of gender, confidence, enjoyment and overlapping barriers such as income, disability and ethnicity. View source details ↓

Resource 5 · StreetGames and Sport Wales

Gwent Peer Research

This source has reasonable currency because it was conducted in 2022 after the pandemic. It is locally relevant because it includes young people in Gwent and pupils at Croesyceiliog and West Monmouth, although the participants were younger than 16–18. Its authority is strong because StreetGames was commissioned by Sport Wales. Its accuracy is supported by trained peer researchers, which may have encouraged honest discussion, but only three groups took part and the findings cannot represent all of Gwent. Its purpose was to understand young people’s preferences and improve local provision through practical action.

How it helps: It gives a local comparison and supports questions about transport, safe venues, friends, commitment and formal versus informal sessions. View source details ↓

Resource 6 · StreetGames and SEWSCAP

Us Girls Wales Peer Research

This source has reasonable currency because the project ran in 2021–22. It is geographically relevant because it concerns girls in South East and Mid Wales, including Blaenau Gwent and Caerphilly, although it does not provide a separate dropout rate for ages 16–18. Its authority comes from StreetGames’ experience of working with lower-income communities and its partnership with SEWSCAP. Its accuracy is supported by an explained peer-research process, but it offers qualitative local evidence rather than a large representative dataset. Its purpose was to understand what girls wanted and fund community action, as well as demonstrate the project’s impact.

How it helps: It provides evidence for possible solutions, particularly involving young people in designing safe, friendly and enjoyable opportunities. View source details ↓

Resource 7 · Sport Wales

Sport and Active Lifestyles 2024–25

This source has excellent currency because it covers 2024–25. It is relevant because it includes ages 16–24 and therefore gives a picture after compulsory school sport, although it cannot isolate 16–18 year olds and measures physical activity as well as sport. Its authority is strong because Sport Wales reports evidence from the Welsh Government’s National Survey for Wales. Its accuracy is supported by clear measures and demographic breakdowns, but the activity is self-reported and the age band is broad for this project. Its purpose is to inform the public, policymakers and investment decisions, while supporting Sport Wales’ aim of increasing activity.

How it helps: It provides a wider Welsh picture of participation after age 16, as long as the 16–24 figure is not presented as a specific 16–18 dropout rate. View source details ↓
